01. Background

Digging past the assumptions

Chase Payment Solutions gives small business merchants a dashboard to accept card payments, issue refunds, and manage disputes.

There were two reasons that led to this project being initiated. The backend was migrating to an order-based data structure, where a sale and its related refund or dispute share a group identifier. And the termination of our WePay vendor relationship gave us three months to bring the entire Disputes experience into Sales Activity.

There was an assumption that the new backend structure should be reflected in the customer facing UI. Before committing three months of design, I tested whether merchants actually thought that way.

They didn't.

02. Problem

Merchants did not understand how payments were related

The legacy table split transactions across two tabs, which were Card Sales and Refunds. Each tab's identification was easily understood, but the relationship between the two was not apparent.

When a merchant asked "where did my payment go?" they had to open one tab, find the sale, switch tabs, find the refund, and hold the reconstructed relationship within their memory. Disputes weren't in Sales Activity at all, they lived in a separate experience entirely, which meant a merchant could miss a chargeback deadline without ever knowing one existed.

Problem Statement

Small business merchants using Chase Payment Solutions need to quickly verify payments. Merchants were forced to mentally reconstruct the story of a related event (i.e. a sale that was refunded) by cross-referencing records across multiple disconnected views. For time-sensitive actions like responding to a chargeback, that friction meant missing a deadline entirely without knowing one existed.

03. Question

The question was whether the merchants' mental model matched the grouped order structure

Based on the new backend order-based structure, a card sale, its refund, and any dispute against it share a common group identifier. The hypothesis was that exposing that grouping in the activity table would make transaction relationships clear to merchants.

Even with a three month deadline, it was important to allocate time for research and testing to avoid rushing a product that doesn't solve the problem.

I built three prototypes, each have a different interpretation of how the transaction's relationships were represented through the design. I tested each prototype against two tasks.

04. Testing

Rejected. Exploration 1: A shared identifier across tabs

If the order number appears on every related record, does the relationship become apparent?

The most conservative direction. I kept the existing tab structure and added a Disputes tab with its own columns: dispute number, reason, date opened, status. Every related record across all three tabs maintained the same order number.

What I learned

An identifier isn't a relationship. The tab boundary was a stronger signal than the repeated number, and it overrode whatever connection the shared ID was meant to communicate.

What happened

Merchants navigated the tabs without difficulty, as that design was the same as before. When they were asked to find the original sale connected to a refund, none of merchants used the order number to do it. They read it as a unique ID per transaction regardless of the fact that it repeated across different types.

Rejected. Exploration 2: Structural nesting

If the relationship is expressed structurally rather than through a shared number, does it become legible?

Parent rows represented the original sale. By expanding a row it revealed child rows for related refunds or disputes transactions. By removing the tabs the shared identifier was not the only way to make the relationship, but not the hierarchy reinfornced it..

What I learned

When merchants expanded the rows, they mistaken the child rows as itemized line items, the components that add up to an order total, rather than separate events that happened later.

What happened

Merchants recognized nested rows through the lens of viewing receipts and invoices, and that prior model was doing the interpreting. This direction also buried the one thing that most needed to be visible: an action-required dispute sitting inside a collapsed parent row.

Pivot : The two explorations failed for the same reason

Both were attempts to make the activity table explain the relationship between transactions. Merchants weren't opening Sales Activity to understand how their order connected across related order events. They were opening it to locate a specific order.

The backend model organizes by orders as groups. Merchants organize by a single event in time. The error was assuming that a structure legible to the system would be digestible in the interface.

→ If either exploration had produced merchants successfully tracing a refund back to its sale, I would have shipped the grouping model. The backend was already built for it and it was the lower-effort path. The research is what took it off the table.

Selected. Exploration 3: Every transaction is its own event

If grouping disappears from the list entirely, does the table match how merchants already think?

Every card sale, refund, and dispute appears as its own row in chronological order. Now the table will remove all tabs and nesting mechanics by the addition of a  Type column to distinguishing. The relational context moves one level deeper into the detail drawer.

What I learned

Merchants found the table immediately scannable and located transactions faster than in either prior direction. Nothing was hidden behind a tab or a expandable row.

What happened

This matched what merchants encounter everywhere else. Other services such as Amazon, PayPal, Shopify, and Square all use order number, transaction ID, and confirmation number more or less interchangeably to mean one transaction corresponds to one number. Merchants arrived with that model already formed. Matching it was cheaper than teaching them a new one.

Order number single row

Solving for the need for action

A dispute isn't just another event in the timeline. If a deadline is missed a merchant loses money.

An account level alert would compete with urgent notifications.  The platform's red dot pattern had already been applied across too many surfaces, and adding another consumer would have further diluted that signal. Dispute urgency had to live inside the context of Sales Activity.

Needs Attention sits above the table as a persistent filter. It's present and subdued when nothing requires action. When a dispute opens, a count badge appears, and the merchant sees it the moment they land on the page regardless of where that dispute falls in the list. Selecting it filters the table to action-required items.

Order number single row: Need attention selected

Relationship context revealed in details drawer

Relationship context now lived where the merchant was ready to recieve the information.

Opening any transaction reveals a status progression bar, full transaction details, and the transaction's history. From a refund or dispute," View original order" takes the merchant to the sale that generated it in one click.

The dispute drawer is the highest-stakes view in the system. Action required appears in red highlighting merchants to the option to fight the dispute.

05. Outcome

System

Single row for each transaction type proved to be the best solution. There were three jobs for a merchant that required three different layers

Activity

what happened?

Chronological list of every transaction, optimized for scanning and retrieval.

Attention

what needs me?

Needs Attention pill, optimized for urgency without competing with the feed.

Context

what does this mean?

Detail drawer, optimized for reconciliation.

Rationale and trade offs

The grouping hypothesis, that exposing the backend order model in the UI would help merchants understand transaction relationships, was disproved.

Merchants think in chronological events, prioritizing merchants' instinct to find the order with one piece of information over the backend's hierarchical structure. Relational reconciliation is supported at the detail screen level, where merchants could make the connection without the added complexity at the activity level.

06. Next

Where I'd take it next

For future iteration, I would enhance the status experience to show a more detailed payment cycle. Additionally, create a high-level dashboard surfacing the metrics merchants check most, and cross-team alignment on the alert system that allows for a more cohesive experience across platform.
